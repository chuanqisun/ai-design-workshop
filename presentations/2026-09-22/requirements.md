SUMMARY OF LECTURE: YOUR SUCCESS IN LIFE BY PATRICK WINSTON

Patrick Winston's lecture argues that professional success is determined primarily by the ability to speak, write, and generate quality ideas. He posits that communication skills are a function of knowledge (K), practice (P), and talent (T), with talent being the least significant factor. Using the analogy of Olympic gymnast Mary Lou Retton being a novice skier, Winston illustrates that knowledge and practice can outweigh inherent talent.

Winston outlines several key heuristics for effective presentation:

1. PREPARATION AND ENVIRONMENT

Rule of Engagement: No laptops or cell phones, as they distract the speaker and audience.

Time and Place: Lectures are best delivered at 11 AM when audiences are alert. The venue should be well-lit (dim lighting signals sleep), cased (visited beforehand to check for technical issues), and reasonably populated (more than half full).

2. STRUCTURE AND DELIVERY

Starting: Avoid opening jokes, as audiences are not yet adjusted to the speaker. Instead, use an empowerment promise, telling the audience what they will know at the end that they didn't know at the start.

Cycling: Repeat key ideas three times to ensure comprehension, as some audience members will inevitably fog out.

Fencing: Clearly distinguish your idea from others to avoid confusion (e.g., My algorithm is linear, unlike Jones's exponential algorithm).

Verbal Punctuation: Use landmarks and enumeration to help lost listeners re-engage with the talk.

Engagement: Ask questions to keep the audience involved, allowing for a standard seven-second pause for answers.

3. TOOLS: BOARDS, PROPS, AND SLIDES

Blackboards: Preferred for teaching and informing because they allow for graphics, match the speed of human absorption, and give the speaker something to do with their hands. This avoids the hands in pockets or hands behind back gestures, which can be seen as concealing weapons or being unengaged.

Props: Effective for empathetic mirroring, where mirror neurons allow the audience to physically feel the action (e.g., a spinning wheel or a pendulum). Winston cites examples from Seymour Papert and Alan Lazarus to demonstrate how props make abstract concepts tangible.

Slides: Best for exposing ideas (job talks and conferences) rather than teaching. Common crimes include:

- Too Many Words: Forces the audience to read rather than listen. Slides should be condiments, not the main event.
- Clutter: Remove logos, titles (if spoken), and background junk.
- Small Fonts: Often used to cram in too much text.
- Laser Pointers: Break eye contact and can be distracting. Use arrows on slides instead.
- Too Heavy: Slides should have air (white space) and imagery.
- Hapax Legomenon: Complex, dense slides can be used only once per presentation.

4. SPECIAL CASES

Inspiring: Passion is key. Winston suggests that inspiration comes from seeing problems in new ways or hearing about what is possible.

Teaching Thinking: Humans are storytelling animals. Teaching thinking involves providing stories, questions, and mechanisms for analysis.

Oral Exams: Failures usually stem from a lack of situating (contextualizing the research) and lack of practice with people who don't already know the work, who may hallucinate missing content.

Job Talks: Candidates have only five minutes to establish two things: Vision (a problem people care about and a new approach) and Proof (that they have done something). Winston uses his own research on human intelligence and symbolic creatures as an example of a stump speech.

5. GETTING FAMOUS AND REMEMBERED

Packaging: Ideas are like children; they shouldn't go into the world in rags.

Winston's Star: To be remembered, work needs:

1. Symbol: A visual icon (e.g., the arch).
2. Slogan: A catchy phrase (e.g., one-shot learning).
3. Surprise: A counter-intuitive element (e.g., learning from one example).
4. Salient Idea: A concept that sticks out (e.g., near miss).
5. Story: The narrative of how it works and why it matters.

6. HOW TO STOP

Final Slide: Should list Contributions, not a long list of collaborators (which belongs on the first slide) or generic Thank You slides.

Final Words: Avoid ending with Thank you, as it implies the audience stayed out of politeness. Instead, use a benediction (e.g., God bless you), a joke, or a salute to the audience, expressing appreciation for their time and engagement. Winston concludes by saluting the audience for valuing the importance of presentation.
